# Independent Analyst Review - B-Catcher Group v7 pack

**B-Catcher Group** | Review of every document in `/TITCH CIP/B-Catcher Group/` (v7) | 23 November 2026
Written as a business, financial and investment analyst would for an investment committee or a seed investor. It is a critique, not an endorsement. Market comparisons use public sources listed at the end. Figures marked "approx." need checking before use.

---

## 1. Verdict in one page

**What it is:** a vertically integrated Australian wholesale private-markets group with three parts. B-Catcher is the manufacturer: it originates, structures and manages trusts. Honey Pot is distribution: brokers plus a direct channel. BHive is the investor platform: onboarding, wallet, reporting, a secondary market and buy-backs. The closest analogues are an Australian unlisted-property and private-credit syndicator (Centuria/Cromwell-style direct funds, Qualitas, Metrics, La Trobe) that has bolted on a Fundrise-style investor app and a PrimaryMarkets-style secondary market.

**Strengths**
- One integrated model drives every document, so the numbers reconcile across the pack. The model recalculates with zero errors, the balance sheet balances, and entity EBITDA reconciles to consolidated.
- Regulatory sequencing is sensible: CAR first, then its own AFSL, a licensed market-operator partner, wholesale only, and gated launches.
- The cost base is lean: 4 employees, everything else variable or outsourced. Fixed cost is low enough that break-even depends on volume, not on hiring.
- The investor fee schedule follows BHive's own model. Measured on gross assets, the management fee is close to market (see section 4).

**The five issues an investor will raise first**
1. **Everything rests on placement volume, and there is no evidence for it yet.** The plan needs $25m in operating year 1, rising to $340m a year by year 5, from a brand-new brand. No signed pipeline, broker letters or track record exist yet. At 70% of the volume (case C below) the plan needs about $2.7m more equity and five-year NPAT turns to -$7.6m.
2. **The buy-back looks profitable on paper only because the model assumes you can always resell at full valuation.** If exercise rises to 15% a year and resale takes 9 months (case A), peak funding jumps from $9.2m to $23.6m and cash goes $11.6m negative. This is the DomaCom/BrickX lesson: liquidity promises fail in exactly the conditions when they are used.
3. **The fee stack is heavy and related-party.** An investor coming through a broker pays about 4.9% up front (placement fee, entry fee and onboarding fee) plus 1.75% a year, all to group companies. That is roughly 2.7% a year over a five-year hold. ASIC's private-credit reports in 2025 and 2026 (Report 820 and Report 846) target exactly this: retained fees, margins and conflicts.
4. **Four employees is credible for cost but not for capacity.** By 2031 the plan launches 58 offers a year, about one a week, and serves 4,365 investors and 160 brokers. The CEO is the only in-house investment person. An investment committee and ASIC will both question resourcing (RG 105 organisational competence).
5. **The valuation is ahead of the evidence.** An $18m pre-money for a pre-licence, pre-revenue Seed is rich for Australia. The exit at a 12x EBITDA / 5x revenue blend overstates quality, because about 63% of 2031 revenue is one-off transaction fees. At 8x / 3x the exit equity value falls by about a third.

**Overall rating** (out of 5): concept 4, regulatory design 3.5, financial model build 4.5, assumption realism 2.5, investability today 2.5. It is fundable as a Seed once the pipeline evidence, fee-stack redesign and buy-back limits below are in place.

These are run through the full model in LibreOffice, in addition to the 8 scenarios already in the pack.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Base (v7)** | $22.5m | $11.6m | $7.0m | $9.2m | $1.28m | Jul 2029 |
| **A. Buy-back run** - 15% of eligible holdings exercise each year, 9-month resale, 3% resale cost | $24.2m | $13.3m | $8.2m | **$23.6m** | **-$11.6m** | Jul 2029 |
| **B. Fee compression** - property placement 2%, PE 3.5%, entry 1.0%, technology fee 0.3% | $18.2m | $8.2m | $1.3m | $10.6m | $1.26m | Sep 2029 |
| **C. Analyst realistic** - 70% of volume, 50% tech overrun, opex +10%, 3-month launch delay | $14.1m | $4.8m | **-$7.6m** | $14.0m | **-$2.0m** | Jun 2030 |

**Read-across**
- Case A is the most important finding in this review. The P&L improves (resale gains) while cash collapses. The model does not let resale prices fall in a run, and in reality they would. A buy-back has to be capped and discretionary, or it needs its own funding line.
- Case B shows the plan survives realistic fee pressure but loses most of its five-year profit.
- Case C is what I would present as the "realistic" case: still viable, but needing about $2.7m more equity (a $15m+ raise) or a smaller build.
- Case D costs about $0.8m of 2031 EBITDA (fewer onboarding fees) but removes about 40 trusts a year of administration, valuations and reporting. I would take that trade.

## 3. Market comparison - where B-Catcher Group sits

| Comparable | What it does | Relevance / lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Qualitas (ASX: QAL)** | Australian private real-estate credit manager. FY25: fee-earning FUM $8.7bn (+28%), base management fees $49m, funds-management EBITDA margin 52% | The plan's 2031 EBITDA margin (52%) matches Qualitas at **17x the AUM**. Margin at $514m AUM is very optimistic. |
| **Metrics, La Trobe, other private-credit managers** | Large wholesale and retail private-credit platforms | ASIC Report 820 (Nov 2025) and Report 846 (Nov 2026) criticise retained borrower fees and margins and weak disclosure, and ASIC will keep watching wholesale funds. B-Catcher's credit product must pass fees through transparently. |
| **Centuria / Cromwell / Charter Hall direct funds** (approx.) | Unlisted property syndicates sold via advisers and brokers | Typical structure is an acquisition fee of about 1-2% of property value and a management fee of about 0.6-1.0% of gross assets. B-Catcher's 1.25% of equity at 45% gearing is about 0.69% of gross assets, so in line. The extra placement fee and technology fee are what make it expensive. |
| **Fundrise (US)** | Retail real-estate and private-markets app | About 1% a year all in (0.85% management + 0.15% advisory). It is the investor-facing benchmark BHive will be compared with: one simple fee. |
| **PrimaryMarkets (AU, owned by ASX: Complii)** | Secondary trading and raises for unlisted securities, including unlisted property units | It already offers "liquidity for unredeemable units". It shows Australian secondary demand exists but volumes are thin. A former ASX executive has questioned the scale of startup secondary markets. BHive's $59m a year traded is plausible only for its own products. |
| **DomaCom (now Assetora) / BrickX** | Australian fractional property platforms | Sources report DomaCom went through administration and restructure. Fractional investors do not hold title. This is evidence that liquidity and platform-failure risk matter to investors. BHive should hold assets in an external trustee and custodian, which the plan already does. |
| **Securitize / ADDX** | Tokenised private-markets platforms | Securitize reportedly had about US$14m of revenue with an EBITDA loss despite more than US$5bn of volume; ADDX raised US$58m. Tokenisation on its own is thin-margin and capital-hungry. Keep tokens as a feature, not the business model, as the plan does. |
| **Birchal / Equitise** (approx.) | Crowd-sourced funding platforms | Success fees of about 5-7% of capital raised are similar to Honey Pot's placement fees, but they are paid by issuers who are not related parties. |

**Positioning conclusion:** the real moat is not the technology. It is the integration of origination, distribution and investor experience under one licence stack, which most Australian syndicators run on separate systems. The pitch should lead with "a better-run wholesale syndicator with its own distribution and an investor app", not "tokenisation" or "marketplace".

## 4. Fee and investor-value review

**Investor all-in cost (property trust, broker-introduced)**
| Fee | Rate | Paid to |
|---|---|---|
| Placement fee | 3.0% of capital | Honey Pot (60% to broker) |
| Entry fee | 1.5% | B-Catcher |
| Offer onboarding | about 0.4% ($20k on a $5m offer) | BHive |
| **Up-front total** | **about 4.9%** | |
| Management fee | 1.25% a year of equity (about 0.69% of gross assets at 45% gearing) | B-Catcher |
| Technology fee | 0.5% a year | BHive |
| **Ongoing total** | **1.75% a year of equity** | |
| Exit (if using the buy-back) | 25% / 15% / 10% below valuation | Group gain |

Over a five-year hold that is about 13.6% of capital, or about **2.7% a year**, before trust-level costs (trustee, custody, valuations, audit).

**Comments**
- "Paid by the issuer" is not a defence when the issuer is a group-managed trust. Economically, the investor pays. Disclose the total cost in one figure on every offer and in the wallet.
- The 0.5% technology fee on top of a 1.25% management fee will be read as double-charging. Consider folding it into the management fee (1.5% all in), or charging it only on secondary-market users.
- The buy-back discount (25% in year 3) is very punitive compared with redemption at net asset value in open-ended funds. Expect brokers to push back. A 10% / 5% ladder with a quarterly cap is more marketable and safer.
- **Investor net returns are missing from the entire pack.** There is no product-level model showing what an investor actually earns (for example a 7% property yield less fees = X% net). Brokers and investors will ask this first. Add a "net return to investor" table for each product.
- Broker economics are thin. 1.8% on about $1.27m a year per broker is about $23k a year per broker on average. Most of the 160 brokers will be part-time introducers. Plan for a 20/80 split, where a few productive brokers drive most volume.

## 5. Financial model review (`BCatcher_Group_Integrated_Financial_Model.xlsx`)

**Build quality - strong**
- 16 sheets, formula-driven, scenario selector and stress levers, zero errors, balance check = 0, intercompany eliminated, entity EBITDA reconciles to consolidated.
- Assumptions are sourced on the Inputs sheet, and set-up and opex registers are line by line.

**Assumptions to challenge**
| Area | Model | Analyst view |
|---|---|---|
| Capital placed, year 1 run-rate | $25m | Possible with 2-3 anchor issuers, but needs a signed pipeline. Without one, use $10-15m. |
| Capital per active broker | $0.4m rising to $1.27m a year | High for introducers; plausible for a few productive brokers. Model concentration. |
| Revenue / capital placed | 5.2% rising to 7.7% | Very high yield. Driven by fee stacking (section 4). |
| Revenue mix 2031 | about 63% one-off (placement, entry, onboarding), 31% recurring, 6% other | Low-quality earnings for valuation. Lead with recurring fees and AUM. |
| EBITDA margin 2031 | 52% | Matches Qualitas at 17x the scale. Use 35-40% in the investor case. |
| Build partner | $24k rising to $32k a month; total platform build $1.43m | Light for a regulated platform with registry integration, mobile apps and a secondary market. Budget $50-80k a month and a $2.0-2.5m build, or cut scope. |
| Buy-back | 4% exercise, 3-month resale at full valuation | Resale price never falls. Add a valuation haircut and exercise caps (case A). |
| GST | Not modelled | Financial supplies are input-taxed, so most GST on costs is not recoverable (partial reduced input tax credits only). This adds roughly 5-8% to non-staff opex. Confirm with a tax adviser. |
| AFSL capital (RG 166) | $750k buffer | Check net tangible assets and cash needs for Honey Pot's own AFSL, and whether the buy-back counts as dealing or principal trading. |
| Wholesale test | $2.5m assets / $250k income | Treasury and ASIC have consulted on raising thresholds. A rise would shrink the eligible pool sharply. Add a stress case. |
| R&D incentive | 30% of BHive staff cost | Small amounts; claims on outsourced development need the group to own the IP and bear the risk. Fine as modelled. |
| Exit valuation | 12x EBITDA / 5x revenue blend gives $172m | At 8x / 3x it is about $116m, and Seed MOIC falls from about 6.2x to about 4x. Show both. |
| Seed valuation | $18m pre-money | Typical Australian fintech Seed: $6-12m pre-money (approx.). Expect pressure; at $10m pre-money, founders keep about 55% instead of 65%. |

**Model hygiene items**
- "Offers launched" shows fractional offers (1.46 in 2027). Round, or show as capital ÷ offer size.
- The Scenarios sheet's "extra equity needed" tests against the $750k buffer only, while the base-case headroom includes the buy-back reserve. Make them consistent.
- The model Read Me says "~38,000 formulas"; the recalculation reports 32,083. Update it.
- The scenarios are a pasted snapshot. Note the date and model version on the sheet, or rebuild them as a data table.

## 7. Top 12 recommendations (in priority order)

1. **Get evidence for volume:** 2-3 signed issuer mandates (about $25m in total) and 10 broker letters of intent before closing Seed. This is the single biggest value driver.
2. **Redesign the buy-back:** discretionary, a quarterly cap of 2-5% of a trust's units, a 10% / 5% discount ladder, buy-backs funded from the trust's own liquidity or a separate liquidity facility rather than group equity, and resale price at the latest valuation less a haircut. Add case A to the model.
3. **Simplify fees:** one investor-facing all-in fee per product. Fold the technology fee into the management fee, cut the property placement fee to 2-2.5%, and publish a total-cost figure.
4. **Add an investor net-return model** for each product and put it in the deck and the IMs.
5. **Lead with the conservative case C** in investor materials and size the raise to it (about $15m, or $12.5m plus a committed bridge).
6. **Move to $10-15m average offers** to cut trust count and administration (case D).
7. **Add two volume-triggered hires:** an investment analyst / asset manager and a fund operations officer. Keep the 4-person core until the triggers are met.
8. **Re-base the technology budget** with two build-partner quotes. Consider licensing registry or investor-portal software.
9. **Model GST leakage** and AFSL financial requirements.
10. **Legal opinions before the Seed:** related-party fees and conflicts, buy-back characterisation, broker remuneration, and digital-asset rules under INFO 225.
11. **Valuation:** a SAFE or note with a cap (about $12-15m) instead of an $18m priced round, and show exit returns at 8x / 3x as well as 12x / 5x.
12. **Deck:** add market size, competition (section 3), team, traction, and a "why investors choose us" slide. Fix the slide 9 percentage.

## 8. Questions an investment committee will ask

1. Which issuers have signed, and for how much?
2. What does an investor net after all fees on a typical property trust?
3. Who decides buy-back pricing, and what happens if 20% of holders want out in a downturn?
4. Why will brokers use Honey Pot instead of HUB24 or Netwealth shelf products?
5. How do 4 people run 58 offers a year and supervise 160 brokers?
6. What is the conflict-management framework when group trusts pay group companies?
7. What happens if the wholesale thresholds rise?
8. What is the minimum raise to reach the month-18 gate if volume is 50% of plan?
9. Who owns the platform IP and code if the build partner relationship ends?
10. How are legacy B-Catcher and BHive shareholders treated, and is the cap table clean?
